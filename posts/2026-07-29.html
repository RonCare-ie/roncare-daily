UK Care Sector Demonstrates Resilience Amid Mounting Workforce Crisis and Rising Demand Pressures

<cite index="39-2,39-3">The UK's residential and nursing care sector has continued to demonstrate resilience through the first half of 2026 despite mounting cost pressures and ongoing workforce challenges, with demand remaining structurally strong due to the ageing population.</cite>

Europe is at a demographic turning point — and the pressure on health and care services is becoming impossible to ignore. The EU is projected to begin a period of steady population decline from 2026, according to Bruegel, a Brussels-based think tank, with the share of people aged 85 and over set to more than double by 2050. That's not a distant problem. In England alone, the number of people aged 65 and over grew from 9.5 million in 2014 to 11 million in 2024, and the care system is already struggling to keep up. For anyone looking after an older parent, a partner, or a friend, these figures tell a story they already live every day.

In England, a July 2026 report from the think tank Re:State has laid out just how stretched the care workforce has become. Vacancy rates stood at 6.8% in 2024–25 — equivalent to around 111,000 unfilled posts — while staff turnover hit 23.7%. Of the care workers who want to leave the sector, 67% say the impact on their health and wellbeing is a key reason, yet only around 16% have ever accessed therapy services, according to The Care Workers' Charity. Re:State is calling for urgent reform across four areas: better use of technology, improved staff wellbeing support, safer working conditions, and a rethink of how councils commission care.

In Ireland, the government has moved to address the gap in services. Minister for Older People Kieran O'Donnell confirmed a €215 million increase for older persons services in 2026 — a rise of over 7% on the previous year. That includes €92 million for the Fair Deal nursing home scheme and funding for 26.7 million hours of home support, an increase of 1.7 million hours. By 2057, one in three people in Ireland will be aged 60 or older. There is still no statutory home care scheme in place, with recruitment and retention in home care described as a crisis.

In Northern Ireland, the over-65 population is set to grow by 44.7% over the next 25 years, according to demographer Ian Shuttleworth of Queen's University Belfast — on top of an existing funding crisis. Across the EU, Southern Europe is the fastest-ageing region, with the very-old-age ratio to workforce projected to triple between 2023 and 2050, according to Bruegel.

For families and unpaid carers, these reports confirm what many already feel — the system was not built for the world we now live in. Care workers are burning out, waiting lists are growing, and too many older people are stuck in hospital or going without the home support they need. Policymakers across the UK and Ireland are being pushed to act: not with vague promises, but with real funding, better pay for care workers, and long-term plans that can survive the next election cycle. The people who need care, and the people who give it, cannot afford to wait much longer.
